Part 1 — the core

The algorithm

Everything else is plumbing. This is the actual idea.

The model

A road network is a graph: 50,000 junctions (nodes) joined by 200,100 road segments (edges). Each edge carries one number — how many milliseconds it takes to drive it. Roads come in three classes at realistic speeds: 90 km/h highways, 50 km/h main roads, 29 km/h village lanes. A road's time is its length divided by its speed. That is the whole model.

Dijkstra, without the maths

Imagine pouring water at a junction. It spreads down every road at driving speed. The moment water reaches a junction you know the fastest route to it, because water always takes the quickest path available and nothing arriving later could have been faster. That is Dijkstra's algorithm (1956).

The safety of it rests on one thing: you always process the smallest time first, so any route arriving later must be longer. The first time a junction is settled, its answer is final.

Step two — "pick the smallest" — is the expensive part. Scanning 50,000 junctions each time would be 2.5 billion comparisons. A binary heap answers it almost instantly. Two refinements matter: when a junction's time improves we push a new entry rather than hunting down the old one (lazy deletion — the wasted work is cheaper than the bookkeeping it replaces), and each heap entry is packed into a single 64-bit integer, time in the top half and junction in the bottom, so comparing two entries is one integer compare.

The insight that makes it fast

The naive approach is to try every pairing:

For each of 200 ambulances and each of 60 hospitals, compute the route from the ambulance to the patient and from the patient to the hospital. 12,000 route calculations. Pick the best.

We measured that. 182 milliseconds per patient. Unusable.

Now look at what is actually being minimised:

total = time(ambulance → patient) + time(patient → hospital) + wait at hospital the blue term never mentions the hospital · the amber terms never mention the ambulance
The separability argument

The two choices are mathematically independent. Which ambulance you send has no effect on which hospital is best, and vice versa. So you do not solve 200 × 60 combinations — you solve two separate, much smaller problems. 12,000 calculations become 2.

Searching backwards

We do not search from each ambulance to the patient — that would be 200 searches. We search outwards from the patient along the roads in reverse. The first free, correctly-equipped ambulance the search reaches is provably the closest, so we stop immediately. One search, not 200. This is why the engine keeps both a forward and a backward copy of the network: twice the memory, 200 searches collapsed into 1.

That early exit is enormous. It settles roughly 359 junctions out of 50,000 — under 1% of the map — before it has its answer.

The trap: waiting time breaks the early exit

Add queue wait and the neat trick above stops being correct. This is the part most people get wrong.

Why stopping at the nearest hospital is wrong

Search outwards and you reach Hospital A after 10 minutes. Travel-time thinking says stop — A is nearest. But A has 40 people waiting, about 90 minutes of queue. Total: 100 minutes.
Hospital B is 25 minutes away and empty. Total: 25 minutes. B wins by over an hour, and you never looked at it.

The correct stopping rule is one line in src/dispatch.c:

if (d >= best_total) break;   /* provably done */

In words: keep searching outward until the frontier is further away than the best complete answer you already hold. At that moment every hospital you have not reached is at least that far in drive time alone, before any queue is added, so none of them can win. Still exact — just more expensive. Measured: 7,631 junctions settled for the hospital search against 359 for the ambulance search. The hospital side was consuming 81% of the total time.

The fix: a hospital distance table

There are only 60 hospitals. So run Dijkstra backwards from every hospital once, in advance, and record the drive time from all 50,000 junctions to each. A table of 50,000 × 60 numbers: 667 ms to build, 11.4 MB to hold.

Answering a query is then not a search at all. It is a scan of 60 numbers:

for each of the 60 hospitals:
      does it have the department?          one AND, one compare
      is a specialist on shift right now?   one AND, one compare
      is a bed free?                        one compare
      is the medicine in stock?             one compare
      total = table[patient][hospital] + queue_wait(hospital)
      keep the smallest
Result

1,314 µs per decision became 50.8 µs — 26× faster, and the answers are identical. Cross-checked on 300 requests against both full Dijkstra and per-candidate A*: zero mismatches.

There is a bonus. Because every hospital is now examined anyway, the rejection list comes out for free — which is exactly what powers the decision log the brief asked for.

The honest cost

The table is only valid while the roads are. Close a road and the table is stale. Closing a road is instant — 107 nanoseconds, because each road stores a pointer to its twin in the reverse network — but rebuilding the table takes 672 ms. For a district where roads close a handful of times a day and thousands of emergencies happen, that is an obviously good trade. For a map that changes every second, it would be the wrong design.

Checking constraints in two instructions

How do you check "does this hospital have obstetrics?" quickly? Not with text — with bits. Each specialty is one bit: trauma 0, cardiac 1, neuro 2, burns 3, obstetrics 4, paediatrics 5, toxicology 6, ICU 7. A hospital's departments are one number, a patient's needs are one number: 

(hospital_departments & patient_needs) == patient_needs

One AND, one compare — two processor instructions, however many specialties exist. No text comparison, no looping, no allocation. The same trick handles ambulance equipment (advanced life support, ventilator, neonatal).

A department is not a doctor

Each hospital carries two such numbers, and the difference matters enormously: spec_mask is the departments the building has at all; on_duty_mask is the specialties with a doctor physically on shift right now. Each of the 420 doctors belongs to a hospital, holds one specialty, and works one of three rotating 8-hour shifts. Every clock tick recomputes it.

This is why routing at 3 a.m. differs from 10 a.m. — and why the decision log can say “no specialist on duty”, a completely different failure from “no such department”. The first fixes itself by morning. The second needs a new hospital.

When everything is busy

Under surge, ambulances run out. Unplaceable patients go onto a waiting list held in a double-ended priority queue (a min-max heap), which can hand you the most or least urgent item equally quickly. Urgency runs 0 (low) to 3 (life-threatening), and the ranking key packs urgency and arrival order into one 64-bit number — so the most critical patient is released first and, among equally critical patients, whoever called first. Nobody is starved. Measured at 27 ns to add, 307 ns to remove over a million entries.

The search horizon

There is a nasty failure mode. When every ambulance is busy, the backward search has nothing to stop on — it sweeps all 50,000 junctions before giving up. Exactly when the system is most loaded, it becomes slowest. The fix is a horizon: stop looking beyond ~15 minutes of drive time.

4,000-emergency surgeTotal time
No horizon29,910 ms
15-minute horizon412 ms — 72× faster
The detail that matters

The horizon applies to the ambulance leg only, never the hospital leg. An ambulance 40 minutes away is useless. But a specialist burns centre 40 minutes away may be the only place that can save this patient. Capping the hospital search would quietly make the system refuse treatable cases — there is a test guarding exactly this.

The full decision, start to finish

1. Search backwards from the patient for the nearest free ambulance
   whose equipment covers what is needed.        ~359 junctions, early exit
   Give up beyond the horizon.

2. Scan all 60 hospitals:                        no graph search at all
      skip: no department / no doctor on shift /
            no bed / no medicine
      score = table[patient][hospital] + queue_wait
      remember every reject and its reason

3. Answer = best ambulance + best hospital,
            both road paths, and the reject list.

4. On COMMIT:  take a bed, join the queue, consume the medicine.
5. On RELEASE: free the ambulance and the bed.
               The medicine stays gone.

Total: about 50 microseconds — roughly 20,000 decisions per second on a single core, scaling nearly linearly across cores because the road network is read-only and shared with no locking at all.

Part 2 — the implementation decision

Why C, and what it actually buys

The algorithm above is the idea. This is why it runs in 50 microseconds instead of 50 milliseconds.

The workload is millions of small, scattered memory accesses per second. At that scale the bottleneck is not how many instructions you execute — it is how often the processor has to wait for memory. C is the language that lets you decide exactly how data sits in memory, and those decisions are the performance.

Four choices you cannot reliably make in a managed language

1 · One struct, not three arrays

Each junction's search state is a single 16-byte record holding distance, generation stamp and parent together, aligned so four of them share one 64-byte cache line. A Dijkstra relaxation touches all three fields of the same junction. Stored as three separate arrays that is three independent cache misses; stored as one record it is one.

2 · Never clearing the distance array

The obvious way to start a new search is to reset 50,000 distances to infinity. That memset alone costs about 50 µs — which would consume the entire query budget before any work happened. Instead every junction carries a generation stamp: a stale stamp means “infinity”. The per-query reset drops from O(V) to O(1) — it is a single counter increment.

3 · One 64-bit integer per queue entry

Each priority-queue entry packs the time into the top half of a 64-bit number and the junction id into the bottom half. Comparing two entries is one integer comparison — about the cheapest thing a processor does — rather than dereferencing two objects.

4 · No allocation in the hot loop

Each thread's 1.14 MB workspace is allocated once at startup. Nothing inside a query calls malloc, so there is no allocator contention and no garbage collector deciding to run in the middle of an emergency. That last point matters more than raw speed: a GC pause is a latency spike you cannot predict or explain, and this system's p99 is a number we have to stand behind.

And the process stays resident

Building the district costs about 12 ms and the hospital table about 670 ms. The engine pays that once at boot and then answers questions on a socket, keeping all state between them. Paying it per emergency instead would be roughly 13,000× slower — which is what a process-per-request design would do.

Being honest about the comparison

We measured C against other algorithms (the table in the next section), not against other languages — we did not build a Python or JavaScript port to race it. The expectation that an interpreted implementation would land in the tens of milliseconds, roughly a thousand times slower, is an estimate from the operation counts, not a benchmark we ran. What is measured is that the memory-layout work above is what took this engine from 1,314 µs to 50.8 µs.

The trade is real and worth stating: C gives no memory safety, so the discipline has to come from testing instead — which is why the suite is 34 assertions run on every build, and why every fast path is cross-checked against an exhaustive method.

What we got wrong along the way

Two optimisations were implemented on intuition — renumbering junctions so nearby ones sit near each other in memory, and reshaping how edges are stored. Both were reasonable. Both delivered approximately zero improvement. The real problem was found only by measuring the ambulance and hospital searches separately, which revealed the hospital side was eating 81% of the time. That single measurement led straight to the distance table and the 26× speedup. Profile first, guess never.

Part 4 — the framing

Why “send them to the nearest hospital” is wrong

The instinct everybody has, wrong for four separate reasons.

1 · The nearest hospital may not do this medicine

A man with a burst appendix does not benefit from arriving at an eye clinic. The nearest building is not the nearest treatment.

2 · The department exists, nobody is on shift

It is 3 a.m. The hospital has a cardiology department. The cardiologist went home at 10 p.m. On paper it can help. Tonight it cannot.

3 · The beds and medicine may be gone

The right doctor and no free bed cannot admit the patient. The right doctor and no adrenaline cannot treat the case they were chosen for.

4 · The queue — this is the big one

A is 10 minutes away with 40 people waiting. B is 25 minutes away and empty. B is more than an hour better.

This is the heart of the project. We are not minimising travel time. We are minimising travel time + waiting time, which is a completely different question and a much harder one.

And nothing stands still

Roads close — a bridge washes out and the shortest route from ten minutes ago is a dead end. Ambulances get used up. Beds get taken. Medicine gets consumed and does not come back on its own. Doctors change shift. Queues grow with every patient you send. So the answer to “where should this patient go” is different every single time you ask, even for two identical patients ten seconds apart.

What “solved” looks like here

For every emergency the system produces:

  • the ambulance to send, and the exact roads it will drive
  • the hospital to take the patient to, and the exact roads for that leg
  • the predicted arrival time and the predicted waiting time
  • a list of the hospitals it rejected and the reason for each — “no such department”, “no specialist on duty”, “no free bed”, “out of medicine”, “unreachable”, “eligible but slower”

That last item is the difference between a system a doctor will trust and a black box a doctor will ignore.

Part 5 — straight answers

Questions you might ask

Including the uncomfortable ones.

Dijkstra is from 1956. What is actually new here?

Dijkstra is the foundation, not the contribution. The contributions are:

  1. The separability argument — recognising that ambulance choice and hospital choice are mathematically independent, turning 12,000 route calculations into 2.
  2. Recognising that queue wait breaks the standard early exit — and deriving the correct stopping rule instead of quietly returning wrong answers, which is what a nearest-hospital system does.
  3. The hospital distance table — 60 precomputed backward searches turn a query into a 60-item scan, giving flat latency from 2,000 to 200,000 junctions.
  4. The ambulance-only horizon — 72× under surge, applied to the response leg only so distant specialist care is never wrongly excluded.
  5. The rejection log — because a system a clinician will not trust is a system nobody uses.

Can you prove it is actually giving the best answer?

Yes, and it is the check we care most about. Every fast path is cross-checked against the slow, obviously-correct method: 300 emergencies against both full Dijkstra and per-candidate A*, with zero mismatches. There is also a test that deliberately constructs a case where the nearest hospital is the wrong answer because of queue wait, confirms the engine picks the further one, and confirms that choice matches exhaustive search.

Is this real data or made up?

Made up, deliberately, from a fixed random seed. A generated 250 × 200 grid with three classes of road at realistic speeds plus bypass links, giving 50,000 junctions and 200,100 segments. Three reasons: reproducibility (anyone can run ./bench and get these exact numbers — with live data no two runs would agree), scale on demand (we can generate 200,000 junctions to test scaling; no public dataset hands you that), and because it is the algorithm being tested — the engine takes junctions, roads and drive times, and does not care where they came from.

The path to real data is short: OpenStreetMap via the Overpass API gives real roads and hospital locations, and India's data.gov.in publishes facility data. Both feed the same graph_build function. That swap is a data-loading job, not an algorithm change.

Have the tests ever actually caught anything?

Yes — and we checked that they can. We deliberately broke the constraint-checking function to see whether the suite would notice. It reported 34 passes. The cause was that the build system did not track header dependencies, so editing a .h file rebuilt nothing that included it — the tests were running against a stale binary. We fixed the build, repeated the sabotage, got the failure we should have got the first time, then undid it. A test suite you have never watched fail is not evidence.

Why should I believe the benchmark numbers?

Run them — make && ./bench, fixed seed, identical dataset every time. The benchmark also reports the things that make us look worse: the 672 ms table rebuild, the 184 MB at 200,000 junctions, the unreachable-route counts, the SLA misses under load. A benchmark that only reports good news is marketing.

Does it really route by type of injury?

Yes, and it is not cosmetic. Each specialty is a bit in a bitmask, and a hospital without the bit is excluded — it never even competes. Ten case types are modelled with their real requirements: a delivery needs obstetrics plus a neonatal-equipped ambulance; a poisoning needs toxicology; a critical transfer needs both ICU and cardiac. In story mode you can watch a nearer hospital get rejected with “has no maternity department” while the patient is sent further. Adding ophthalmology or orthopaedics is one more bit each — the checking code does not change at all.

Why does the time of day matter?

Because a department is not a doctor. Each of the 420 doctors belongs to a hospital, holds one specialty, and works one of three rotating 8-hour shifts; every clock tick recomputes which specialties are actually staffed. So a hospital can have a cardiology department and still be the wrong answer at 3 a.m. The decision log distinguishes “no such department” from “no specialist on duty” — the first is a permanent fact about the building, the second fixes itself by morning. Conflating them would mislead whoever reads the log.

What happens when everything is busy?

Patients go onto an urgency-ranked waiting list (a double-ended priority queue). The most critical patient is released first; among equally critical patients, whoever called first, so nobody is starved. Every time an ambulance is released the list is drained in that order. Under a 4,000-emergency surge with the horizon on, the whole thing processes in 412 ms.

What if a road closes mid-emergency?

Closing a road is 107 nanoseconds. The next decision immediately routes around it, and if a hospital genuinely becomes unreachable it is rejected with the reason “unreachable” rather than silently dropped. The distance table needs a 672 ms rebuild to stay exact. There is a test that closes every road into a junction, checks nothing is reachable, reopens them, and verifies the decision comes back identical to before.

Where does the medicine go?

It is used. Committing a case decrements the hospital's stock permanently. Releasing the case returns the ambulance and the bed but not the medicine, because the patient took it. Stock only rises via an explicit restock. There is a test for exactly this, because “resources that quietly regenerate” is the most common way a simulation flatters its own numbers.

Why C? Isn't that an odd choice in 2026?

Because the workload is millions of small, scattered memory accesses per second, and C is the language that lets you decide exactly how data sits in memory. Those choices are the performance — the full case, with the four specific decisions and what each one bought, is in Why C above. Short version: 50 µs decisions in under 20 MB, with no allocation in the hot loop and therefore no garbage-collector pause landing in the middle of an emergency.

Would this actually work in a real district?

The routing core, yes. What would need real-world work: real map data (straightforward, described above); live traffic — the engine already stores a base drive time separately from the current one precisely so a traffic multiplier can be applied, but the plumbing to push live updates in bulk is not written; live hospital status — beds, staff and stock are modelled correctly but fed by simulation, and in reality would come from hospital systems, which is an integration and policy problem more than a technical one; and the 672 ms table rebuild, which would need incremental rebuilding if the map changed constantly.

What is the one thing you would show me?

Story mode, one cardiac arrest, at 3 a.m. You watch the two nearest hospitals get rejected — one has no cardiology department at all, one has the department but the cardiologist went home — and the patient gets sent to a third hospital further away that can actually treat them. Every step is on screen in plain English. That single case contains the entire argument for why “send them to the nearest hospital” is the wrong answer.

Part 6 — reference

Glossary

Graph
Junctions (nodes) joined by roads (edges). How a computer stores a map.
Dijkstra's algorithm
The 1956 method for finding the fastest route from one point to everywhere else.
Priority queue / binary heap
A structure that instantly answers “what is the smallest item right now?”
Lazy deletion
Pushing an improved entry instead of hunting down the old one, and discarding stale copies when they surface.
Separability
Two choices that do not affect each other, so they can be solved independently rather than in combination.
Early exit
Stopping a search the moment the answer is provably final.
Distance table
Precomputed drive times from every junction to every hospital, turning a search into a scan.
Bitmask
A set stored as bits in one number, so membership tests are single instructions.
Horizon
A cap on how far the ambulance search will look before giving up.
Min-max heap
A queue that can produce the most or least urgent item equally fast.
p50 / p90 / p99
Percentiles. “p99 = 361 µs” means 99 of 100 decisions finished within 361 µs.
µs / ns
Microsecond = one millionth of a second. Nanosecond = one billionth.
